*Between Two Fires* (2012) is a historical-horror novel set in France in 1348, during the Black Death. A disgraced knight escorts a girl who believes angels have given her a mission in Avignon. The journey reveals that the plague is part of a war between Heaven and rebellious angels.

These are plot-recall notes, not a review. **Full spoilers, including the ending, follow.** The headings below describe events in sequence rather than pretending to reproduce every chapter; the novel itself has titled chapters.

## Sequence of events

### The war behind the plague

The opening establishes a second rebellion by Lucifer and the fallen angels. War, famine, and pestilence weaken humanity while God seems absent. The demonic campaign is not merely an outbreak of ordinary cruelty: the invaders want to overturn Heaven, and the plague is one weapon in that struggle. This cosmic frame makes the grotesque encounters on the road part of a single conflict.

### Thomas finds Delphine in Normandy

Thomas is a former French knight who has become a brigand. Traveling with other desperate men through a plague-stricken countryside, he meets **Delphine**, a young girl whose father has died. When his companions threaten to sexually assault her, Thomas turns against them and saves her. She insists on burying her father and then traveling with Thomas. She claims to see angels and to receive guidance from the dead. Her destination is **Paris, then Avignon**, the seat of the papacy.

Thomas is violent, cynical, and reluctant to take responsibility for her, but his decision to protect her is the first sign that his old sense of knighthood has not disappeared. Delphine's innocence is not naivety: she sees dangers and spiritual realities that the adults cannot.

### The ruined priest and the river creature

In **St. Martin-le-Preux**, the travelers meet **Père Matthieu Hanicotte**, a priest whose parish has collapsed amid plague and shame. He offers hospitality and tells Thomas about a monstrous thing in the river, which has become a place of death after plague corpses were thrown into it. Thomas fights the enormous eel-like creature; its horrible human characteristics and ability to mimic a victim's cries make clear that the supernatural is breaking through into everyday life. He kills it but falls gravely ill with plague symptoms.

Delphine directs them to a shrine of the Virgin, where rival villagers fight over a sacred statue. The statue is broken during the violence, but Delphine uses a fragment of it to heal Thomas. Matthieu joins the journey. The trio becomes the novel's central traveling company: a knight who doubts goodness, a priest who doubts his worthiness, and a child whose faith is inseparable from terrible knowledge.

### The castle feast and Thomas's disgrace

On the road, Thomas and Matthieu encounter a seemingly thriving castle offering a feast and a tournament. Its hospitality conceals corruption and demonic cruelty. Delphine refuses to join the revelry; Thomas's instincts as a fighter help him recognize the danger. The encounter makes the supernatural threat feel like a perversion of familiar medieval rituals—feasting, chivalry, religious ceremony—not simply monsters lurking in wilderness.

Thomas eventually tells Matthieu what happened to him at the **Battle of Crécy (1346)**. He was wounded while fighting the English, and during his absence **the Comte d'Évreux** seized his lands. Thomas's wife **Marguerite** ended up with his rival, and Thomas was excommunicated. His bitterness is not abstract: he has lost his home, marriage, social identity, and place in the Church. Revenge against d'Évreux has sustained his life as an outlaw.

### Paris: the relic and the living statues

In Paris, the group shelters with **Jehan**, a woodcarver, and **Annette**. The city is hungry, frightened, and beset by nightmarish visitations. Delphine and Thomas encounter a seller of religious relics who is persecuted by a crowd; after they help free him, he gives Delphine a **spearhead identified with the lance that pierced Christ**. The relic becomes important protection against demons.

At Jehan's house, ominous knocking and the sound of a crying child draw the household toward the door. A monstrous animated **statue of the Virgin Mary**, carrying a dead infant, attacks and kills their hosts. The sacred image has been turned into an instrument of horror. The spearhead drives it away, and the survivors escape the city. Other church statues appear similarly corrupted. Paris illustrates the novel's recurring terror: familiar Christian signs have become unstable, and people cannot tell at a glance what is holy and what is profane.

### Matthieu's confession and the temptation in the barn

Matthieu confesses the history of his disgrace. He loved a young man named **Michel**, was discovered, and was publicly humiliated; the scandal estranged him from his parish. His shame and his failure to minister to people during the plague torment him. Thomas reacts with discomfort and prejudice, but their companionship continues. Delphine's acceptance contrasts with the cruelty Matthieu has experienced.

A demon later attacks Thomas through a dream or vision, trying to twist his protective love for Delphine into sexual violence. Thomas resists the compulsion. This matters to his character: redemption is not the disappearance of temptation but the decision not to yield to it. Smaller signs of grace also punctuate the journey, including the suggestion that life returns to a dead tree near Delphine.

### The angelic battle and the road south

At **Auxerre**, the travelers witness a tremendous confrontation between supernatural beings. The battle is physical and devastating, and the heavenly side can be wounded. The spectacle confirms that the angels are not effortlessly winning and that the struggle has spilled into human geography.

The group heads south toward Avignon by water. Matthieu also faces a temptation tailored to his guilt: a deceptive holy-looking figure offers a vision of a conventional domestic life if he turns against Delphine. He refuses. The book repeatedly tests the three travelers through the things they most desire—restored honor, absolution, safety, or the appearance of an ordinary life.

### The river of corpses and Matthieu's death

Traveling along the **Saône and Rhône** waterways, the company encounters grotesque threats, culminating in a barrier of corpses and monstrous floating heads or creatures with stinging appendages. Their vessel is overwhelmed. Matthieu protects Delphine, holding her above the danger while he is fatally stung.

They reach shelter, but Matthieu dies. His death is one of the journey's major sacrifices: a man convinced that he is stained and unworthy gives his life to save the girl. Delphine cannot simply undo his death. The story gives his choices weight rather than treating his past shame as the final definition of who he was.

### Delphine leaves; Thomas confronts d'Évreux

Fearing that everyone who protects her will die, Delphine goes ahead alone. She faces further demonic deception while Thomas searches for her. During the search he encounters **d'Évreux**, the man he has long wanted to kill. Their confrontation is interrupted by violence involving English soldiers, and d'Évreux dies. The encounter does not restore Thomas's former life or heal the damage done by his obsession.

Delphine obtains access to the papal court through d'Évreux's identity and papers. Before they enter Avignon, she requires Thomas to confront his hatred of **Marguerite**. Forgiveness—not defeating his rival—is the decisive step. Delphine baptizes Thomas, and he is transformed to resemble d'Évreux so they can approach the palace.

### Avignon and the false pope

In Avignon, Delphine seeks **Pope Clement VI**, but the man exercising papal power is a demonic impostor. They also meet **Robert**, Matthieu's brother, who is connected to a cardinal and the papal household. Robert betrays Delphine's plans to the false pope, placing Thomas and Delphine in danger.

Delphine discovers the body of the **real Clement VI** and brings him back to life. The real pope confronts the impostor, whose identity is revealed as **Baal-Zebuth**. The conflict turns into a supernatural assault on Avignon. Thomas is killed; Delphine is also killed while protecting the pope. Her death releases or reveals a heavenly host, including **Michael**, and the demons are defeated. Robert also dies amid the destruction.

The climax resolves the book's central mystery: Delphine is not merely a child who receives angelic visions. Her identity is bound to **Christ's return**, and her apparent vulnerability is central to how the heavenly victory becomes possible.

### Thomas in Hell; the altered return

Death does not end Thomas's story. His soul is carried to **Hell**, where he experiences torment. Delphine comes for him in a scene echoing the **Harrowing of Hell**, Christ's descent to liberate souls. She reveals that she is both the Delphine Thomas knew and a manifestation of Christ. Thomas is rescued.

The world is restored in a way that erases most people's knowledge of the cosmic battle. Thomas returns to the point near the beginning of his journey and again encounters Delphine after her father's death. She no longer remembers what they endured together, but he does. This time he helps bury her father and continues caring for her. The return is not simply a reset: Thomas carries the moral change of the first journey into his second chance.

### Epilogue: Thomas's later life

Years later, Thomas has become a **Franciscan friar**. He visits the castle and speaks with Marguerite, but does not try to reclaim the life he once thought he could only recover through vengeance. He then goes to see **Delphine**, now a nun and his adopted daughter. The ending leaves him with a different form of family, service, and peace from the lordship he lost.

## Places, history, and religious references

- **1348 and the Black Death:** The historical plague provides the setting and, within the novel's supernatural premise, part of the demons' assault on humanity.
- **Battle of Crécy (1346):** Thomas's military defeat and injuries precede the loss of his lands and excommunication.
- **Normandy → Paris → Auxerre → Rhône valley → Avignon:** Broad route of the pilgrimage, with detours and river travel.
- **Avignon papacy:** The pope is based in Avignon rather than Rome, which makes the city's papal palace the destination.
- **Holy Lance / spearhead:** A relic associated with the piercing of Christ's side; used against demonic manifestations.
- **Saint Sebastian and the Virgin Mary:** Christian figures and images associated with healing and protection, sometimes grotesquely imitated or corrupted by evil.
- **Harrowing of Hell:** Traditional Christian account of Christ's descent among the dead; mirrored by Delphine's rescue of Thomas.
- **Franciscan life:** Thomas's eventual vocation, contrasting with the status and violence of his former knighthood.

## Things worth remembering

1. The novel begins with a war in Heaven and ends with a version of Christ's descent into Hell; Thomas's road journey connects those two scales.
2. Thomas's first act of rescue is violent, but his ultimate transformation is his willingness to forgive.
3. Matthieu's sacrifice contradicts his own belief that his shame makes him unworthy.
4. The horrors repeatedly invert sacred forms: feasts, tournaments, saints' statues, relics, clerical authority, and even the pope.
5. Delphine's mission is not merely to expose a false pope; she also changes Thomas, and his remembered second chance is the book's final consequence.
